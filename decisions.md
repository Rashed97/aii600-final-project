## 2026-10-28: Forecast weekly flu admissions in Virginia to book surge nursing shifts

- Decision. The quantity is confirmed influenza admissions to Virginia hospitals in the coming week, from the CDC NHSN state-week series, with the NSSP emergency-department flu visit share as a leading indicator. The decision is how many surge nursing shifts a hospital system books a week ahead, scored as a newsvendor cost in shifts and dollars.
- Why. The series is public domain, so it can go on the `data` release; it has six full seasons (2020–21 through 2025–26) with peaks from 49 to 1,315 admissions a week, so there is both a strong seasonal signal and a held-out season that never enters fitting; and the decision has an asymmetric loss (understaffing costs more than an idle shift), so the action rule is a quantile, not the mean, which is the course's threshold-from-loss idea. A known provenance issue, the May–November 2024 voluntary-reporting window where as few as 26 hospitals reported, gives a concrete bias-versus-noise discussion.
- Alternative. Forecast morning departures at one Capital Bikeshare station to decide overnight rebalancing. Same decision shape and far more rows, but the data license forbids redistributing the files, the decision maker's costs are harder to pin down, and 300 weekly points we can name beat 600,000 rides we cannot.
- Revisit. If the statewide count turns out to be too coarse to stand in for one hospital system (for example, if the NSSP health-service-area series shows the Northern Virginia peak lands weeks away from the state's), switch the quantity to the Fairfax–Arlington health service area's ED visit share and rebuild the loss around that. If the 2024 reporting gap cannot be rescaled defensibly, fit on 2022–23 and 2023–24 only and say so.
- AI use. Claude Code drafted this entry and the proposal from the instructor's lab page and the two CDC feeds; the data pulls and the counts above were run and checked by the tutor session.

## 2026-11-11: Freeze both CDC feeds as of 11 November 2026 and keep every column

- Decision. The analysis runs on one snapshot of the two CDC feeds, taken 11 November 2026 with `fetch_data.py`, whose last week ends 31 October 2026. The files are on the `data` release, their SHA-256 hashes are in `data/README.md`, and `flu.verify_data()` stops the notebook if a file is missing or differs. The files are the source rows for Virginia with every column (322 and 20); choosing and renaming columns happens in `flu.py`, where it can be read. For the emergency-department series the statewide row (`county = All`) is the default.
- Why. Both sources are revised every Wednesday and the hospital feed is labelled preliminary. In this snapshot the hospitals reporting over the last four weeks are 91, 91, 87, 82, so the newest counts are incomplete and will change. Reading a moving source would change the held-out scores from one run to the next. Keeping the raw rows means no choice about the data is hidden inside the download.
- Alternative. Read the API live each time the notebook runs: always current, but no result could be reproduced and the last weeks would change under us. Or store only the six columns `flu.py` uses: smaller files, but it would hide columns a later entry may want, such as the admissions per 100,000 and the share of hospitals reporting.
- Revisit. Take a second snapshot before the final checkpoint. Comparing the two measures how much the last few weeks get revised, which is an error a real one-week-ahead forecaster faces and this snapshot cannot show. If the revisions are large, the baseline and the model have to be scored on what was known at the time. Switch the emergency-department series to the Fairfax-Arlington health service area if the quantity moves to Northern Virginia (see the 28 October entry).
- AI use. Claude Code wrote `fetch_data.py`, `flu.py`, the setup, question and data cells of `analysis.ipynb`, and this entry. The numbers quoted are printed by the committed notebook.

## 2026-11-18: Hold out every forecast for weeks ending on or after 1 July 2025

- Decision. A forecast is held out when its target week ends on or after 1 July 2025: the whole 2025-26 season and the first 18 weeks of 2026-27, 70 forecasts, 5 July 2025 to 31 October 2026. The cutoff is a date; no random draw is involved. Nothing is fitted, scaled, or tuned on those rows.
- Why. The decision is about a later time, so the held-out data is a later time. The instructor's note on the proposal asked for the 2025-26 season. Adding the 2026-27 weeks gives 18 more test weeks and a second autumn rise.
- Alternative. Hold out the 2025-26 season only (52 forecasts). It avoids the newest weeks, which are preliminary: hospitals reporting fall from 91 to 82 over the last four weeks of the snapshot, so those counts will be revised.
- Revisit. If a second snapshot shows the last weeks were revised by more than the model's typical miss, score the 2026-27 weeks on what was known at the time, or cut them from the held-out set.
- AI use. Claude Code implemented the split in `flu.weekly_frame` and drafted this entry. The choice between the two alternatives was ours.

## 2026-11-18: Fit on the three seasons from October 2022, without the voluntary-reporting weeks

- Decision. Training forecasts have target weeks from 8 October 2022 to 28 June 2025. The 2020-21 and 2021-22 seasons are not used. Forecasts that touch a voluntary-reporting week (May to October 2024) are left out of the fit, which removes 27 of 143, leaving 116.
- Why. The pandemic seasons peaked at 49 and 57 admissions a week against 495 to 1,315 since; they describe a flat season that the decision will not face, and the ED series only starts in October 2022. For the voluntary weeks the evidence conflicts. The reporting hospitals held about 39% of the state's inpatient beds (6,400 against 16,500 to 18,700 in other summers), which says the count is a fraction. But admissions per point of ED flu share were 132, inside the range of the other summers (103 to 201), which says the count looks normal. We cannot tell which is right, so the fit uses neither reading.
- Alternative. Rescale those weeks by the share of hospitals reporting: it gives 341 admissions per ED point, far above every other year, so it overcorrects. Or use the counts as reported: that asserts they are complete, and the bed counts say they are not.
- Revisit. Leaving out those summer weeks lowered the fitted sigma to 0.284, and the 80% interval then covers 69% of held-out weeks. Compare the three treatments by leaving one training season out at a time; if the as-reported weeks give intervals that hold up, use them. A second source for summer 2024 admissions would also settle it. The treatment must not be chosen by the held-out score.
- AI use. Claude Code ran the check, recommended dropping the weeks when we asked, and drafted this entry. We first chose to rescale and changed our minds on the check.

## 2026-11-18: Model next week's log admissions on this week's admissions, the ED flu share, and a yearly wave

- Decision. log Q(t+1) = b0 + b1 log A(t) + b2 log E(t) + b3 sin + b4 cos + error, with Normal errors of one standard deviation sigma, fitted by least squares on the training forecasts. A(t) is admissions in the week just ended, E(t) the share of ED visits diagnosed as flu, and the sine and cosine are one yearly wave in the day of the year. The forecast is lognormal and the booking is one of its quantiles. Fitted values: b1 = 0.697, b2 = 0.189, sigma = 0.284.
- Why. Logs, because flu changes by a factor from week to week and the spread grows with the level. On the held-out weeks at the headline cost ratio, a regression on last week's admissions alone costs $105,994 a week, adding the ED share $101,680, and adding the yearly wave $98,854, against $106,908 for booking last week's count.
- Alternative. The same regression without the yearly wave: simpler and one fewer thing to learn from three seasons, but $2,826 a week worse. Booking last week's count, with or without a margin: no model, and $8,054 a week worse.
- Revisit. The single sigma is the assumption we trust least: training misses have sd 0.23 in the middle third of weeks and 0.30 in the low and high thirds, and out of sample the median covers 66% of weeks where it should cover 50%. If the residual plots show the error depends on the level or the season, let sigma vary or add a term for the direction of last week's change. The model also lags the December 2025 rise.
- AI use. Claude Code proposed two model forms, wrote `flu.design` and `flu.fit_ols`, and drafted this entry. We chose the form with the yearly wave.

## 2026-11-18: Loss: correct the booking quantile; a staffed unit costs $650 and a shortfall three times that

- Decision. The cost of booking K when Q admissions arrive is c_shift K + c_short max(Q - K, 0). The booking that minimises it is the quantile 1 - c_shift/c_short of the forecast, which corrects the proposal: it gave c_short/(c_short + c_shift), and that does not follow from this cost. One unit of capacity is the nursing for one flu admission: a 3-day median stay (CDC FluSurv-NET 2024-25, MMWR 74(34)), one nurse to five patients (Cal. Code Regs. tit. 22, section 70217, as a reference standard), and $45.12 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, registered nurses, Virginia). That is 14.4 nurse-hours, 1.2 twelve-hour shifts, and c_shift = $650. We assume c_short = 3 c_shift = $1,949, so the booking quantile is 0.667.
- Why. One more unit costs c_shift for sure and saves c_short only when Q exceeds K, so the best K has P(Q > K) = c_shift/c_short. The ratio of 3 stands for a shortfall that means a boarded or diverted patient, not only overtime. It is an assumption; nobody measured it.
- Alternative. A ratio of 1.5 to 2, a shortfall covered by agency or overtime staff at a premium: the rule then books at or below the median (89 to 101 admissions a week against 114 at a ratio of 3). Or a ratio of 5 (128 admissions). The notebook's sensitivity table shows all four; the model beats last week's count at each, by $6,089 to $20,942 a week.
- Revisit. A hospital system's own figures for agency rates, diversion losses, or total cost per nursing hour replace these. The $650 counts wages only, so it is a floor. The action changes direction at a ratio of 2.
- AI use. Claude Code found the error in the quantile it had written into the proposal, looked up the three sources, recommended the ratio when we asked, and drafted this entry.
